Models of network formation, who befriends whom, who trades with whom, are notoriously expensive to fit. The likelihood involves a sum over every possible network, so exact computation is hopeless beyond a handful of nodes. A variational method published in the Review of Economics and Statistics promised estimates at a fraction of the usual cost.

Before a method like that becomes standard, someone should check it on the models people actually use. I built a simulation framework that pits the variational estimator against the established alternatives on networks with realistic structure, including the clustering you see in real social networks, at sizes from 50 nodes to 10,000.

The fast method failed on both counts. Its estimates piled up at extreme values while the standard approaches stayed centered on the truth, and it was slower too. On the 10,000-node network it had not finished after two weeks of runtime; a sampled pseudo-likelihood estimator got accurate answers in about two hours. Sometimes the most useful result is a clear warning label.
